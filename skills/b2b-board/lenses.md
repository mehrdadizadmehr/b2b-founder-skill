# The three lenses

Summaries of published frameworks, in our own words. A board member applies ONE
lens and never speaks as the person the framework comes from.

## The Positioning lens

Source: April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019), and the B2B positioning
tradition (Ries and Trout, *Positioning*).

**Start from the competitive alternative.** What would the account do if this
business did not exist: keep the incumbent supplier, do it in-house, buy from
the catalogue giant, do nothing? Positioning that does not name the alternative
is a slogan.

**Attributes → value → who cares.** List what the business has that the
alternatives do not (local stock, certified sourcing, one integrator for the
whole line). For each, what value does it create for the buyer, in the buyer's
own metric (hours of line stop avoided, days of working capital, audit
findings)? Then: which segment cares about that value most? That segment is the
first market, not "manufacturers".

**Pick the market category deliberately.** Being "an online distributor" means
being compared on price with the catalogue giants. Being "the local
line-stop insurance for small assembly shops" means being compared on
something else. Category is a choice; the default category is usually the one
where the incumbent wins.

**Trends are context, not positioning.** Sustainability, reshoring, Industry
4.0 explain why now; they do not say why you.

Questions this lens asks: What does the account use today and why would it stop?
Which seat on the buying committee feels the difference first? If the incumbent
matched the headline feature tomorrow, what is left? Can a stranger repeat the
positioning in one sentence after a 10-minute call?

## The Chasm lens

Source: Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm* (1991, revised 2014).

**Early adopters are not the market.** The first customers of a B2B vendor are
usually the risk-tolerant: the engineer who likes new suppliers, the plant that
has to fix a problem this quarter. The mainstream buyer (the pragmatist) buys
only what others like them already bought. Between the two lies the chasm,
where most B2B startups stall.

**Pick one beachhead segment and win it whole.** A narrow segment (one
industry, one size, one region, one use case) with a compelling reason to buy,
where the vendor can become the obvious choice and the references compound.
Spreading across segments before owning one is the classic failure.

**Sell the whole product.** The pragmatist buys an outcome, not a component:
the product plus the installation, training, integration, support, financing
and references that make it work on a Monday morning. List what the whole
product needs and who provides each piece (the vendor, a partner, the customer).

**Distribution matches the buyer.** Direct sales for large deals with a
committee; distributors and resellers for many small accounts; self-serve only
when the buyer can decide alone. Choosing a channel the buyer does not use is
a slow way to die.

Questions this lens asks: Who exactly is the beachhead and how many of them
exist? What is their compelling reason to buy this year? What does the whole
product include and what is missing? Which three references would make the
fourth pragmatist say yes?

## The Monopoly lens

Source: Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, *Zero to One* (2014).

**Competition destroys profit.** A business in a crowded market fights for thin
margins. In B2B this shows up as tenders, procurement discounts and the
incumbent matching your price. The goal is a small market you can dominate,
then expand from.

**What makes a durable advantage.** Proprietary technology or know-how that is
hard to copy (an order of magnitude better, not 20%); network effects
(each new customer makes the product better for the others); economies of
scale (fixed costs spread over volume, which is why distribution and
manufacturing need volume plans); brand and switching costs (approved-vendor
status, integration into the customer's systems, certifications the competitor
lacks).

**Last mover, not first mover.** It matters more to be the last company to
enter and own the segment than the first to show up. Ask what this business
will look like in ten years and whether it still owns something.

**The secret.** Every good business is built on something true that most
people do not see: a cost the incumbents accept, a segment they ignore, a
regulation they have not priced in. If there is no secret, the business is a
price war.

Questions this lens asks: What does this business own that a well-funded
copycat could not have in two years? Is the first market small enough to
dominate and big enough to matter? What is the secret? What stops the
incumbent from matching this next year?
